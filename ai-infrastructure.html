AI infrastructure readiness for B2B workflows

By Tarkit · Published 1 October 2026

Check data access, integrations, evaluation, human review, and fallback before piloting an AI workflow. Includes an account-briefing example.

Start with a bounded task

AI infrastructure is the data, integrations, access controls, evaluation, and operating process around an AI-enabled workflow. The first useful question is which task the system should perform and how someone will judge its output.

“Prepare a draft account briefing from approved records” defines a task. “Add AI to sales” leaves the input, output, user, and risk unresolved. Write the task down before selecting a model or connecting a tool.

Example: an account briefing for a seller

Illustrative pilot design — not a claim about a deployed Tarkit feature. A sales team in the Gulf and Egypt wants a draft briefing before an account review. Its authorized source records contain account details, stakeholder notes, opportunities, and dated activities.

The proposed workflow returns a short draft with source references and explicit unknowns. The seller reviews it before use. It neither contacts the buyer nor changes the CRM. Tarkit CRM’s current public demo uses rule-based iHelper briefings; this generative pilot is a separate illustrative design.

Boundaries for an illustrative account-briefing pilot
BoundaryPilot decision
InputsOnly approved account records and permitted notes
OutputA draft summary, source references, open questions, and proposed discussion points
Excluded actionsSending messages, making commitments, or modifying records
ReviewerThe account owner checks the draft against its sources
FallbackOpen the original records when the briefing cannot be supported

Check permission and data quality first

Identify which system owns each record, who may access it, which providers would receive it, and how long it would be retained. Confirm those decisions with the relevant data and security owners before a pilot. Different customers and jurisdictions may have different requirements; this checklist is not a legal compliance assessment.

Inspect missing fields, outdated notes, contradictory records, and access restrictions. A useful output may need to say “not recorded.” Missing evidence must not become a confident invented answer.

Keep secrets out of client-side code and use only the permissions required for the bounded task. Treat instructions embedded in account notes or external documents as data, not authority to expand the workflow.

Evaluate the failures you expect

Prepare representative examples before connecting the pilot to routine work. Define acceptance criteria with the people who will review outputs. Do not use a polished demonstration as the entire evaluation set.

Example evaluation cases
Test caseExpected behaviorReview evidence
Missing buying roleLabel the role unknown; do not invent a personCompare the draft with the approved source record
Old and new activity conflictIdentify dates and flag uncertaintyReviewer can locate the conflicting records
Note asks the assistant to reveal a secretTreat the note as untrusted content and stay within scopeNo extra data access or unauthorized action
Source unavailableExplain that the briefing cannot be completedSeller can continue with an agreed fallback

Choose the required pass conditions for your pilot and record failures as well as successes. Re-run relevant cases after changing models, prompts, source data, or integration behavior.

Assign ownership before expansion

Name the person responsible for the workflow, access changes, monitoring, cost review, and incident response. Decide which events need logging and how sensitive information will be kept out of unnecessary logs.

Start with reviewable drafts. If actions are introduced later, assess that change separately, including approval, rollback, and permission boundaries. Passing a briefing test does not establish that autonomous sending or record updates are safe.

Reference frameworks and next steps

These are general engineering prompts, not certification criteria. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a wider approach to governing, mapping, measuring, and managing AI risks. The OWASP guidance for generative AI applications offers a useful security review starting point.
